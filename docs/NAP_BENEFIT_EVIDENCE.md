# SleepBank — Nap-Benefit & Sleep-Debt Evidence Base

> **Purpose.** A durable, citable record of *why short naps are worth taking* —
> the evidence on subjective alertness/sleepiness outcomes and the honest
> relationship to sleep debt — for future presentation or journal write-up.
> Compiled from a fact-checked deep-research pass (2026-06-09): 5 search angles,
> 18 sources fetched, 78 claims extracted, 25 adversarially verified (3 independent
> verifier votes each), 24 confirmed / 1 refuted. Confidence and vote tallies are
> noted per finding. Companion to `EEG_EVIDENCE.md` (EEG staging evidence) and
> `RATIONALE.md` (clinical rationale).
>
> **Verify every citation against the primary source before any external/clinical/
> regulatory use.** Small-n studies and commonly-repeated figures are flagged.

## 1. Headline: short naps reliably restore *subjective alertness*, but do **not** repay sleep debt

The peer-reviewed evidence strongly supports brief naps (~10–20 min) as an
effective countermeasure that **quickly restores subjective alertness and reduces
sleepiness for ~1–3 hours**. The cost side is **sleep inertia** — post-wake
grogginess — which grows with nap duration as naps reach slow-wave (N3) sleep.
The ~10–20 min window is the practical sweet spot: fast, clean recovery with
minimal inertia. *(high confidence.)*

Crucially for SleepBank's **"descriptive, not a ledger"** design: naps restore
acute alertness **without erasing accumulated sleep debt**. This is the central,
defensible framing — see §4.

## 2. The dose–response curve (subjective alertness & sleepiness by nap duration)

| Nap "dose" | Subjective/alertness effect | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 30–90 s (ultra-brief) | **No measurable benefit** | below the effective threshold |
| **10 min** | **Immediate** improvement across sleepiness, fatigue, vigor, cognition; lasts ~**155 min** | the single best "clean" recovery |
| **20 min** | Benefits **delayed** (emerge ~35 min post-wake), last ~**125 min** | trades immediacy for a brief inertia tail |
| **30 min** | Brief **sleep inertia** first, then improved performance up to ~155 min | reaches N3 → grogginess cost |

*(high confidence, 3-0.)*

- **Brooks & Lack (2006), *Sleep*** — THE canonical duration-comparison.
  Repeated-measures crossover, **n=24** healthy young good-sleepers/non-nappers,
  nocturnal sleep restricted to ~5 h, afternoon naps at 3:00 PM of EEG-verified
  **5/10/20/30 min of sleep** + a no-nap control, 3 h of post-nap testing (windows
  at 35, 95, 155 min). Subjective sleepiness via **SSS/KSS** plus fatigue/vigor and
  cognitive/vigilance tasks (DSST). The **10-min** nap won for immediate
  cross-outcome recovery; the **5-min** nap gave little; the **20-min** delayed
  onset; the **30-min** showed the inertia cost. *(3-0.)*
- **Tietzel & Lack (2002), *J Sleep Res*** — within-subjects, **n=16**, 5 h
  restriction; only the **10-min** nap helped — **30-s and 90-s naps did nothing**.
  Establishes a **~10-min minimum effective dose**. *(3-0.)*
- **Hayashi, Watanabe & Hori (1999), *Clin Neurophysiol*** — a single **20-min**
  nap at 14:00 improved subjective sleepiness, performance, and **self-confidence
  in task performance**. *(3-0.)* **Caveat: n=7 and the subjects were rested, not
  sleep-restricted** → supports an *acute alertness/vigilance-maintenance* benefit,
  **not** debt repayment. The self-confidence outcome is directly relevant to
  SleepBank's "reported feelings" framing.
- **Milner & Cote (2008), *Sleep Biol Rhythms*** — **n=32** across young/middle/
  older adults; a **20-min** nap improved subjective sleepiness, fatigue, and
  accuracy, with **no age × benefit interaction** (older adults didn't need to nap
  longer). *(3-0.)* Caveat: one secondary snippet suggested the 60-min nap drove
  the sleepiness drop — the duration-specific decomposition may be nuanced.
- **Lovato & Lack (2010), *Prog Brain Res* 185:155–166** — review: brief naps
  (5–15 min) give **almost-immediate** benefits lasting a limited **1–3 h**; longer
  naps (>30 min) impose inertia first, then longer-lasting cognitive gains. The
  **"1–3 h"** is the standard citable duration-of-benefit figure (primary studies
  put it at ~125–155 min). *(3-0.)*

## 3. Operational evidence — the NASA cockpit nap (cite the numbers precisely)

**Rosekind et al. (1995)** — "planned cockpit rest," long-haul pilots. A **40-min
rest *opportunity*** (the planned window, **not** realized sleep); the Rest Group
(**n=12**) fell asleep in **5.6 min** and slept **~25.8 min** (the popularly cited
"~26-min nap"). Continuous ambulatory EEG/EOG, PVT-style vigilance, actigraphy,
subjective ratings. The nap improved physiological (EEG) alertness and vigilance
vs the No-Rest control (n=9), sustained through descent/landing. *(3-0.)*

> **Cite the numbers correctly** *(2-1 — the load-bearing numbers traced to and
> matched the primary NASA source; the dissent was only about the "accuracy"
> wording):*
> - **~54%** = improvement in **physiological (EEG) alertness** (fewer
>   microevents/microsleeps). ✓
> - **~34%** = **reduction in reaction-time/vigilance *lapses*** in the final
>   ~90 min of flight — **NOT** "34% better flight-task accuracy." The common
>   "accuracy on flight tasks" phrasing is a **secondary-source embellishment**;
>   correct it whenever cited.
> - Defensible wording: *"~54% improvement in physiological (EEG) alertness and
>   ~34% fewer reaction-time/vigilance lapses."*

This is also a **direct alertness-vs-debt dissociation** in an operational study —
see §4.

## 4. Sleep debt — the honest picture (this anchors "descriptive, not a ledger")

Two converging lines show naps **restore acute alertness without discharging
accumulated homeostatic sleep debt**:

1. **NASA (Rosekind 1995):** the cockpit nap improved alertness yet explicitly
   **"did not affect... the cumulative sleep debt."** A direct, operational
   alertness-vs-debt dissociation. *(3-0.)*
2. **Mechanism (Lovato & Lack 2010):** brief-nap benefits — from naps containing
   **virtually no slow-wave/N3 EEG** — are **"not predicted by the... homeostatic
   sleep drive (Process S)."** They propose an alternative **"Process O"**
   (sleep-onset / sleep-switch) mechanism. *(theoretical claim, 2-1.)*

> **Frame the Process S/O point as a leading hypothesis, not settled fact.** It is
> the researchers' active theoretical interpretation. Converging support:
> **Stepan et al. (2021)** found cognitive protection during sleep deprivation
> tracks the **amount of slow-wave sleep** — tying true sleep-pressure relief to
> N3, which brief naps lack. So a brief nap restores alertness *because* it isn't
> discharging the homeostatic process.

**Bottom line:** a ~10–20 min nap transiently restores subjective alertness and
reduces sleepiness; it does **not** mathematically erase sleep debt. SleepBank's
sleep-bank is therefore a **descriptive tally**, not a quantitative ledger — the
science says a ledger would be pseudo-accounting.

### 4a. Cycle naps and sleep debt — a *different* transaction than the power nap

The "no debt repayment" finding above is specific to **brief naps that contain
virtually no N3**. A **~90-min Cycle Nap** is a fundamentally different physiological
transaction, and the same mechanism that explains why the power nap *doesn't* repay
debt predicts that a full-cycle nap *partly does*:

- **N3 is the currency of homeostatic discharge.** Slow-wave activity (concentrated
  in N3) is the physiological index of **Process S** dissipating (Borbély two-process
  model — see `RATIONALE.md §2`). A ~90-min nap completes a cycle with **substantial
  N3 plus REM**, so it **genuinely discharges accumulated sleep pressure** — exactly
  what the power nap deliberately avoids. Stepan et al. (2021) tie real
  sleep-pressure relief to the **amount of slow-wave sleep**, which the cycle nap has
  and the power nap lacks.

So the two nap types are **two different transactions**, not just short vs long:

| | Power Nap (~10–20 min) | Cycle Nap (~90 min) |
|---|---|---|
| Sleep architecture | mostly N1/N2, ~no N3 | full cycle: N3 + REM |
| Discharges Process S (sleep debt)? | **No** | **Partly — yes** |
| Primary value | transient alertness, fast & clean | real restorative sleep, memory consolidation |
| Sleep inertia risk | minimal | higher if mistimed (wake from N3) |

**But three caveats keep even the cycle nap *descriptive, not a ledger*:**
1. **Partial, not a substitute.** A cycle nap repays *some* pressure; you cannot
   nap your way out of chronic restriction. Naps supplement nocturnal sleep, they
   don't replace it.
2. **It partly borrows from tonight.** Discharging sleep pressure in the afternoon
   lowers the *nighttime* sleep drive — a long/late nap can blunt or fragment that
   night's sleep (the basis of standard sleep-hygiene advice against long afternoon
   naps for poor sleepers). So it is partly recovery, partly time-shifting.
3. **Not quantifiable to the minute.** The discharge depends on prior sleep
   pressure, circadian phase, and how much N3 was actually obtained — "90 min napped
   ≠ 90 min of debt repaid."

> **Open question (needs a targeted research pass for hard numbers):** the canonical
> duration studies in §2 tested only **≤30-min** naps. The *magnitude* of sleep-debt
> repaid by a daytime ~90-min cycle nap — and how much it costs subsequent nocturnal
> sleep — is not pinned down here from primary sources. The N3→Process-S reasoning
> is well-established; the specific daytime-cycle-nap recovery figures are not yet
> cited.

**Design implication:** SleepBank should frame the two naps honestly by their
*kind* of value — Power Nap = "wake up sharper" (alertness, no debt claim, minimal
grogginess); Cycle Nap = "actually get some sleep" (restorative N3/REM that does
discharge real sleep pressure, at the cost of ~90 min and a borrow against tonight).
Neither is a numeric ledger entry.

## 5. Sleep inertia — the cost side of longer naps

- **Stage at waking tracks inertia** (simulated night shift, **n=31**, PVT-B,
  PMC4763354): a **30-min** nap reached **~14.7 min SWS** (~80% woke from SWS) and
  impaired response speed at **all** post-nap points (still worse at **47 min**);
  a **10-min** nap (~0.8 min SWS, ~80% woke from Stage 2) caused **no significant
  impairment**. *(3-0.)* Caveat: the authors couldn't statistically prove the
  stage→inertia link ("tracks" = data pattern, not proven causation); nighttime
  study, so daytime magnitudes may differ.
- **Magnitude/duration (Hilditch & McHill 2019 review; Jewett 1999; Bruck &
  Pisani 1999):** severe inertia resolves within **~15–30 min**, mild residual
  impairment can last **an hour+** (addition-task recovery to **~3.5 h** in one
  study); decision-making can remain **~20% below optimum at 30 min** post-wake.
  *(3-0.)*
- **The SWS→inertia link is genuinely contested** — some studies show ~41%
  performance drops from SWS awakenings, others find none. So **SWS is not a clean
  predictor of inertia.** *(3-0.)*

> **⚠️ Refuted reasoning — do NOT use (0-3):** *"A ~20-min nap avoids slow-wave
> sleep because SWS doesn't start until ~30 min in."* SWS can begin earlier and the
> SWS→inertia link is contested. **Justify the ~10–20 min recommendation by the
> demonstrated immediate-benefit / low-inertia *data*, not by an SWS-timing
> mechanism.** (This is exactly why SleepBank times waking off *detected* onset and
> deep-sleep approach rather than a fixed clock assumption.)

## 6. The cognition meta-analysis (don't over-read the "duration doesn't matter" null)

**Leong et al. (2023), *Ageing Res Rev*** — meta-analysis of **60 samples / 54
studies**: afternoon naps give a **small-to-medium overall cognitive benefit
(d=0.379)**; largest for **vigilance (d=0.610)**, then procedural memory (0.494),
declarative memory (0.376), processing speed (0.211). Reported **no significant
moderation** by duration, age, timing, habituality, or prior sleep restriction.
*(3-0.)*

> **Two caveats before citing the moderation null:**
> 1. This pools naps and measures **cognition**, **not** subjective sleepiness or
>    inertia — for which duration **demonstrably does** matter (§2, §5). Do **not**
>    state "duration is proven not to matter."
> 2. The moderation null is **likely underpowered** (sparse subgroup cells =
>    absence of evidence, not evidence of absence); the authors themselves hedge.

## 7. Subjective measurement instruments (what "reported feelings" means here)

For an in-app "how do you feel?" rating, the standard, citable self-report
instruments are:
- **Stanford Sleepiness Scale (SSS)** — 7-point.
- **Karolinska Sleepiness Scale (KSS)** — 9-point.
- **Visual-analog scales** for mood / fatigue / vigor, and self-confidence ratings.

Objective alertness in this literature is the **Psychomotor Vigilance Task
(PVT/PVT-B)** (reaction-time lapses), plus cognitive tasks (serial
addition/subtraction, DSST) and decision tasks (e.g., "Fire Chief"). *(synthesized
from the instruments named across the verified studies.)* SSS/KSS are the
defensible choice for SleepBank's post-nap self-report.

## 8. Open questions (gaps a study would need to fill)

1. Do **repeated/habitual** short naps over days/weeks move cumulative
   sleep-debt **markers** (next-night architecture, PVT trends, melatonin/cortisol),
   or only give a one-off acute boost? Verified studies measured **single** naps —
   SleepBank's over-time value is unaddressed.
2. Optimal nap **timing** vs circadian phase / prior wake duration, and how much
   timing modulates both benefit and inertia (most studies fixed ~14:00–15:00).
3. How the **1–3 h** benefit and inertia profiles translate to genuinely
   **sleep-deprived / shift-working** populations vs the mildly-restricted (5 h) or
   rested young adults that dominate these studies.
4. Direct head-to-head: does an **N1-only / sleep-onset** nap (the Process O /
   sleep-switch idea) match or beat a brief N2 nap for subjective alertness? This
   would tell us whether to target a **stage at wake** vs a fixed clock duration —
   directly relevant to SleepBank's smart-alarm design.

## 9. How SleepBank applies this (design decisions)

- **Defensible product claim:** *"A ~10–20 minute nap reliably and quickly
  restores subjective alertness and reduces sleepiness for ~1–3 hours."* No
  sleep-debt-repayment claim.
- **"Descriptive, not a ledger":** the sleep-bank is a plain tally — the evidence
  (§4) says naps don't quantitatively repay debt, so a mathematical ledger would be
  pseudoscience. (See memory `sleep-bank-descriptive-not-mathematical`.)
- **Default Power Nap (~18 min post-onset)** sits in the demonstrated
  immediate-benefit / low-inertia window; the smart alarm exists to wake **before
  N3 consolidates** — justified by the inertia *data* (§5), not an SWS-timing myth.
- **Post-nap self-report:** use **SSS/KSS** (§7) as the in-app "how do you feel"
  instrument — and as an outcome measure for our own validation study (does
  onset-timed waking reduce inertia vs a fixed timer?).
